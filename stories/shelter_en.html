Shelter

I raise my head towards the cold winter sky. A red signal light blinks against the backdrop of distant stars. I shudder. I look away. I focus on the touch of your body. Its warmth. You press closer, as if you sensed my distress. "At least a few things will be alright," whispers a long-unheard, well-familiar voice. It spreads through me like a xanax pill. "At least a few things will be alright." "At lea-" It falls silent. The cold sky pulls me toward it like a tractor beam. I see the signal light beneath my feet. I no longer see you beneath it. The xanax feeling fades. Planet Earth beneath me fades. Awareness of Planet Earth's existence fades. My legs fade. There are only freezing stars. Then only frost. Then nothing.

***

"Crrrr." I jerk my head in confusion. "Crrrrrr." Ah. Ugh. I don't like that sound. "Alice, give me another half hour." "According to previous instructions I'm not allowed to." "I have override permission." "Aren't you spending that a bit cheaply? You only have two left." "No, let me lie here." I gave Alice precise instructions on how to manage my daily rhythm in this timelessness, with only a few permitted refusals. Sometimes I don't like myself for it, but I know it's necessary. To hell with it. I roll from side to side, the soft foam sinking under my back, inwardly grateful for the anti-gravity device. In my head, the warmer part of the dream mixes with the dread at the end. I curl up against the pillow and try to return through meditation to the feeling of your touch. It doesn't work; gradually everything leaves my mind and in the end I just lie there, unaware of time passing.

"Crrrr." "That's half an hour, okay, Alice, enough, I'm getting up." I pull on a t-shirt and sweatpants, lazily prepare some corn flakes, Europa's Best brand, Europa Cinnamon and Europa Apple flavor. Made in Europa. Everything here is Made in Europa. For a few more years I can still live here like a student in some old American film. By my life's standards, that's not bad. The morning routine continues with a visit to the control room and its view into the empty depths of space.

The stars are so clear from here. Blacker black and brighter white. "So many stars that I could not for the life me understand how the sky could contain them all yet be so black," I recall from a book. Among them flickers the blinking red dot of Bob's signal diode. Bob and Alice — the ship's satellite and computer — my only friends for kilometers around. Unlike Alice, Bob doesn't say much. He reminds me of a large telecommunications tower from my childhood. It stood alone in a green meadow, its artificiality in dissonance with the harmony of nature, like a remnant of some alien civilization. Just as I too often felt like an intruder among all those effortless people whose lives seemed to run themselves, I sometimes felt more kinship with it than with any of them.

I've been looking at the stars and their cold glow, filled with indifference, every day for years now. It used to frighten me, but the more exhausted I grew from the cruelty of the world, the more soothing that coldness became. Sometimes I get the feeling it doesn't even bother me anymore. Contact with the outside world is now maintained only through the signal picked up by Bob. I lower my eyes to the terminal sitting before the viewport into the void. It reads December 21, 2056, 10:30 in coordinated Earth time. "Alice, daily report," I request. Alice keeps a table of captured message statistics for me. Once they paint a more positive picture, maybe I'll start thinking about returning. "Sentiment index plus 0.0017 points," Alice shares — within the range of random noise. But the increase over a period corresponding to a few Earth months seems to be a signal, however weak, as does the greater instability. Even though the barometer of human consciousness still reads low, perhaps - if only perhaps - something small is beginning to happen. Who knows.

I forget all of it and my sluggish morning thoughts wander to you again; today it has apparently decided you'll be the gravitational center of their drifting. I start imagining a music club. A mass of people in the grip of collective euphoria, carried by the undulations of air rippling with music. Us among them. And then I feel like living. Hooray, my Waves are back. "Waves" is what I call my occasional bursts of hypomania. I love lying with them, tucked up with headphones. I let the sound waves of music navigate my inner Waves of emotion, surrendered to being carried in reverie anywhere through the space of human experience. From a distance, without having to take part. That's my way of living here.

So - back to the cabin. Back to the duvets and pillows and soft foam. I have a copy of a hacked Spotify library from sometime around 2025. I thought I might pick up something newer through Bob, but we never captured a music signal here. We are, after all, in the back of beyond. I don't feel like anything specific anyway, so I set it to shuffle, put my headphones on, close my eyes and, head on the pillow, set out to surf the Waves.

***

As I'm just approaching the crest of one, I'm gazing out the window toward the horizon between the purple neons of the surrounding skyscrapers. The first rays of morning sunlight, softened by the fragrant smoke of the club, gently caress my face. Tiredness in my legs after a night of dancing. Something heavy and painful happened last night. But right now we're here. The glossolalia of Elizabeth Fraser vibrates through the air, every syllable of her voice saying "Everything will be alright," in a language that speaks to the part of us that existed before we had words for loss or suffering. Like the Genius Loci itself placing its hand on my shoulder in a comforting touch. The girls are just coming back with another round of neon drinks...

What painful thing happened? That's not really the point - in the Waves it's about letting the pure emotion wash through you; when you shift the effort toward imagining something concrete it falls apart like a rainbow if you tried to touch its end. Riding a Wave to its highest point without breaking it is a kind of meditation that I actively practice here.

As my stream of consciousness turns away from experiencing toward analyzing the nature of Wave-surfing, I realize that was today's peak - the next ones will only be smaller and smaller. Reality seems to suddenly lose its sharpness, as if the awareness descended on me that the previous peak was just another copy of something already experienced many times. I hug the pillow with a sinking feeling of futile longing to return somewhere I can no longer go.

After maybe ten, maybe twenty minutes, as I'm slowly preparing to return to the daily routine, a gentle acoustic guitar melody catches my ear. Followed by a voice that is in no way inferior to it. It sings, whispers of closeness. "Alice, what is that?" "Blossom by Porter Robinson." Oh. With "I had lost my sight, so you sang to me the beauty you'd been seeing, if I can't stop time, I'll build a world where God cannot take us, there's no need to think of time" something in my head shifts this time. The feeling of relief from your touch in the morning dream suddenly returns. As if this new Wave not only reached the same heights, but was somehow also "deeper." Quick. Where is it? Third drawer of the wardrobe. I pull out a pale pink cube I've named Shelter, hold it in my palms and concentrate on my emotional meditation as best I can. It's easier not to break it when it relates to a specific memory.

That night on Earth, the night before we left for the empty depths of space. The night when I mentioned that with all the resources we had, we could build so much more, if only we could dare to dream of big things. You nodded, swept a chestnut strand of hair falling across your eye, and then spoke with new enthusiasm in your voice about your own visions. About what imprisons human imagination, what stops us from dreaming of worlds where God won't have power over us. And I felt for the first time in my life that someone truly understood me. That I had met someone who had developed what I only had vague intuitions about. As if all the tenderness and beauty you saw in your visions had lifted me above the banality of all the planes of life I had known until then. As if it longing to carve my ambitions into the matter of the Universe for the first time. "So you sang to me the beauty you'd been seeing." I wanted to find words that would express what it is like to be understood for the first time in your life, but nothing could convey the feeling itself. Everything sounded flat. If only I could short-circuit the sheer necessity of expressing myself through language. If only I could convey the feeling directly, I thought to myself back then. I'll have at least this version, I whisper to myself, aware of the gentle coolness of the Shelter's walls against my palms. If we ever see each other again, and I manage to get it to work. Then I ask Alice to play the song again. I repeat this three more times, then put Shelter back and go to sink into other memories in bed. I want to remember how it all went wrong.

***

It was after we'd been posted to Jupiter's moons. Me on Europa, my Adina on Ganymede. You received a call-up for military service in the Jovian Solidarity faction, and I wanted to be near you, so I signed up for military training and then for the Outer Statutes unit, on a mission exploring the ruins of the Euritan civilization on the neighboring moon. It was more of a desperate move — in the strict environment of the exploration and colonization paramilitary units we couldn't see each other much. And then tensions began forming between them on top of that. They grew and grew like water behind a dam in a flood, and I realized I had to do something. I tried to argue for diplomacy and de-escalation. It was useless. I longed for some device, something that would convey to those dimwits what we had said to each other that night on Earth. But my words weren't enough. I didn't feel I should be considered a hero despite all that; it wasn't my fault no one listened to me. I was again as separate from everyone except you, like that radio tower from nature in childhood, and so I decided to save the only thing that mattered to me.

I didn't hold out much hope of surviving the battle. And at night I was haunted by the image of us looking at each other with blasters aimed into each other's eyes, knowing only one could survive. I couldn't contact you — all outgoing channels from Europa were filtered. Yet I was secretly preparing an escape. Once the battle was over, I'd already be gone, and then I'd contact you from a location outside Europa.

I was packing some things when the bombs started falling. It was a few days earlier than I'd expected — and I had expected them earlier than everyone else. So I quickly grabbed the bag I'd prepared with things for us. The survival necessities were already loaded into the speeder, thankfully. I had scouted out this abandoned station as a place sufficiently removed from civilization that no one would bother to look for us here, while at the same time giving me the possibility of contact under a false identity. After landing I opened the bag, and looking at that strange cube instead of the expected treasure, I felt a wave of disappointment. I had planned to steal the Euritan coronation scepter, which would have given us enough on the black market for a comfortable life for several decades, if not enough to found our own faction. And what was this — some pink cube? What good was that supposed to be to us?

Still, I tried to contact you. I received no reply. Through intercepted messages I knew you were safe. I assumed you had ultimately disagreed with the theft and the desertion. Or perhaps my feelings were more one-sided than I'd thought. You were the first person who had ever understood me, but what did I mean to you? How could I even know? What if I'd made it all up entirely?

A wave of cold suddenly passes through my body. The one from the morning dream, when the tractor beam in the sky separated me from you. It passes through and retreats again. Or so I think.

***

With the inner weather slowly returning to the everyday, I sit on the edge of the bed and toss Shelter from hand to hand, as if I want to juggle with it. Strange and a little tragic how poor our language is for expressing emotion — for expressing the most elemental aspect of our lives. I recall how we once talked about their philosophy.

"They're like colors," I argued. "Except colors can be described using combinations of red, green and blue, but 0.7 sadness, 0.3 longing can't express what the 'In September' sample in Burial's track Raver stirs in me. What do you think — could you do something similar by mapping brain receptor activation?" I asked.

"I don't know, that probably won't be enough. Similar to how knowing the composition of tones at a single moment tells you little without having heard the preceding ones." You answered mildly, then smiled as if you wanted to cheer me up and added, "Imagine the graffiti - Adina 0.7 serotonin-2A 0.3 GABA-B Julie."

***

"Julie, run to the control room, I have a message you have to hear!" Alice's excited voice cuts through my tired earlobes. Occasionally she receives something from Bob that she thinks I should hear in person, not just as part of the statistics. And she'll only tell me in the control room — she says it's the least boring thing here and that she needs at least something to demonstrate a flair for drama. She says she needs to feel like an individual at least for a moment. "Okay, if you insist," I sigh.

So I shuffle over there, Shelter still in my hands, the starry sky and Bob's diode in front of me. "You have to hear this!" Alright, alright. "In a routine official communication, someone forgot to delete the detail that the communications of a certain Aidan Zaida from the base on Ganymede were placed under military surveillance five years ago today." "So?" "Think about it — I'm certainly not going to tell you right away." Looking at the stars and the blinking red light of the diode in front of me, the morning dream flashes through my mind, and with it you at the center of gravity of my attention. Five years ago? That would fit. The emotional resonance that your voice created in the dream flickers through my body again. But who is Aidan Zaida? Am I just reading things into this? Isn't it just "when all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail"? I start trembling.

"I can see you understand, but are you alright? I didn't expect such a vague hint to upset you like this," Alice says.

"Who is Aidan Zaida?" I ask.

"It's an anagram of Adina Adiza, of course," Alice replies, taking the trouble to simulate concern in her voice.

With the realization I turn away from Bob and the stars in front of me, and now it fully sinks in just how much I've missed this feeling. A tremor of real hope has a different color than reverie and Wave-surfing and memories, which have been my only life here. I grip Shelter tightly in my hands.

Bob among the freezing stars welcomes me again when I raise my head a few minutes later. I look away from them as from something I hope to never see again. I focus on the coolness of Shelter in my sweaty hands. Shelter is like a camera for inner states. I remember how I first understood what it was really for.

In my disappointment over losing the scepter, I had found a use for the cube at least as something to grip in my hands during my Waves, like a stimming toy. Once I was seized by a longing to re-experience a previous Wave, then that same disappointment at not being able to return to it. And with that longing it washed over me — and I felt it. Exactly the same. As if that very longing were the key to replaying what I'd inadvertently recorded by gripping it in my hands. Then I gave Alice the task of keeping a database of all my "recordings" with Shelter, and I recorded everything I longed to convey to someone, or to preserve just so I could return to it someday.

I've only managed to play it back a few times. But I'll figure out how it works. Out there, with the help of people smarter than me.

"I lost my sight, so you sang to me the beauty you'd been seeing," plays in my head. And now I have it here, in Shelter — an entire personal museum of what I want to convey back to you. That was what I wanted, and gradually, in the creeping comfortable laziness here at the station, I was forgetting it. The Waves cannot be my whole life. They're not enough if they're not shared, if they're not part of something larger.

"Really, Julie, are you sure you're alright? It's still just another vague hint — you've never reacted to them this strongly before." Alice asks, emotionally intelligent enough to take her time with the question.

"Alice, my dear, I think it's more that once you fully remember again that you don't want to fall in love with radio towers and satellites, there's no going back."
